THE INVESTIGATION / 2026

First, the polls were questioned.
Then, the votes arrived.

Before Election Day, questions about polling came from two directions: the authenticity of surveys that appeared in election databases, and methodological decisions that could change reported margins. A fake survey is a failure of data provenance; an authentic survey that misses its target is a different problem of measurement. This project measures the latter and documents both.

OCTOBER 2026

What counts as a credible poll?

After Wedgewood Polls acknowledged fabricating surveys, trackers—including The New York Times—removed its data. The Times published an editors’ note about its vetting process. A warning label is not the same as independent verification.

03 / WHAT THESE NUMBERS CAN AND CANNOT SAY

A warning about trust. A test of accuracy.

The 2026 polling story begins with two different questions: Were public surveys genuine, and did genuine surveys accurately measure the electorate? Reporting about fabricated polls raised concerns about the first. Pre-election discussion of weighting adjustments raised questions about the second.

This interactive audit addresses a narrower, measurable question: among contests with comparable public surveys, how far did the polling margin differ from reported election results? The data alone cannot show why an error happened or establish that a particular weighting change caused it.

Selection

Baseline uses questions ending October 4–November 2, 2026, excludes flagged partisan/internal surveys and known fabricated pollsters, and prefers likely-voter samples. Original survey questions are deduplicated.

Calculation

Both polling and results margins are normalized to the major-party vote: 100 × (D−R)/(D+R). Signed error = election margin − polling margin; absolute error is the magnitude.

Two evidence tiers

Headline summaries require two distinct pollsters in a race. Exploratory figures include single-pollster contests and are not equivalent evidence.

Limitations

Most districts lack qualifying polls, the dataset is not a random sample, candidate lineups can be unusual, published questions may be correlated, and the election returns are not certified.

The finding is conditional on the sample.

In the strict 30-day dataset, the qualifying polls tend to understate Democratic margins, but the count of qualifying races is small. The same data explorer reveals a broader and less uniform set when a single-pollster comparison is permitted or when partisan surveys are included. Neither finding represents the full universe of House and Senate races.

More complete vote certification, late House survey releases, and manual source reconciliation are still needed before a final causal or industry-wide conclusion. These are limitations of the evidence, not reasons to hide the measurements.
